## Mon Sep 21 — ✍️ CV Fix #3 · "How far back should your work history go?"

---

The most common CV question from people with 20+ years of experience:
"should I remove my older jobs?"

Short answer: mostly yes. And it feels awful, so here's the reasoning.

Your CV is not your career record. It's an argument for one specific job.
A hiring manager filling a warehouse team-lead role needs to see your last
decade of relevant work — not your first job from 1996.

A practical rule:

- Last 10–15 years (or last 3–4 roles): full detail
- Earlier: one line each — title, company, years
- Very early or totally unrelated: a single line, "Earlier experience in X
  and Y available on request"

Why this isn't erasing your experience:

- The depth is exactly what your bullets from recent years show. Twenty
  years of doing the work shows in HOW you describe it, not in the date of
  the first job
- Older detail invites the exact bias you fear: "won't be cheap", "set in
  their ways", "overqualified". A tight CV controls that first impression
- If they want the full history, they'll ask — at interview, where you're
  a person instead of a stack of pages

The one exception: keep older roles if they're your proof for the target
job. Changing to a career you had 15 years ago? That "old" job goes back in,
with detail.

The experience isn't wasted. It's just not all needed on page one.

## Tue Sep 22 — 🎯 Should I Apply? #1 · "The ad says 5 years. You have 3."

---

The job ad says 5 years' experience. You have 3. Do you apply?

Apply. Here's the maths nobody tells you:

That "5 years" is a wish list, not a password. Hiring managers write the ad
for a perfect candidate who does not exist, then hire the best real person
who applies. Nearly everyone hired for a role was missing something from
the ad.

What actually decides it:

- The core 2–3 requirements — can you genuinely do the work? That's it.
- The rest — tools you can learn, "nice to have" extras, years-of-experience
  arithmetic — is negotiation room, not gatekeeping

Read the ad twice and sort every requirement into two piles:
- "The job is doing X every day" → you need real evidence
- "Familiarity with X / knowledge of X preferred" → nice, not required

If pile one is solid, apply. Let them decide. Rejecting yourself is free for
them and expensive for you.

When NOT to apply (because this isn't "spray everything"):
- When pile one has a hole in its centre — you'd be misrepresenting
- When the ad is clearly a senior step with real responsibility you've never
  carried
- When you'd be one of 400 identical applicants with zero hook for the role

Fewer, better-targeted applications beats a hundred maybes. But 3 of 5
years? That's a maybe you take.

What's the requirement you saw recently that made you not apply? Ask below
whether it was real or a wish.

## Wed Sep 23 — 🎤 Interview Q of the Week #3 / 💰 · "What salary are you expecting?"

---

The question people fear more than any other. Some preparation, because it's
a negotiation, not an exam.

Before the interview:
- Know the actual market for the role in THAT country/city. Check union
  salary stats (Sweden publishes these), Glassdoor, job ads that do show
  bands, and ask people in the field
- Decide your three numbers: the "I'd be delighted" number, the "fine, let's
  do it" number, and the walk-away number

In the room, if you can, deflect once:
- "I'd rather understand the role fully first — could you share the range
  budgeted for the position?" A good interviewer answers this. It's normal,
  not rude.

If they push for a number:
- Give a range, not a point: "Based on the market and the scope, I'm looking
  at X to Y." Put your real target near the bottom of your stated range
- Never name a number below your walk-away, hoping to seem flexible

Sweden specifics: with a kollektivavtal the band may be set, and the
negotiation is smaller — but individual top-ups, overtime terms and
qualifications increments are real things you can discuss.

The biggest mistake isn't a wrong number. It's having never decided the
number before the question lands.

Have you ever lowballed yourself and realised later? What did it teach you?

## Thu Sep 24 — 🧠 Transferable Skills #3 · Casino → what it qualifies you for

---

Worked in a casino? You have a skill set most office workers genuinely
don't. Nobody believes me, so here it is written down.

Casino work, translated:

- Handling money and chips under camera, with zero discrepancy tolerance →
  cash handling, reconciliation, integrity under observation
- Watching a floor and noticing what's off → risk awareness, surveillance
  instincts, attention to detail. Security teams pay for exactly this
- Following strict gaming regulation to the letter → compliance experience.
  That word alone gets you interviews in finance and insurance
- De-escalating a drunk, angry high-roller without an incident → conflict
  resolution at a level customer service training only dreams of
- Running a table through a full shift, in front of everyone, mistakes
  visible instantly → performance under sustained pressure
- Supervising dealers, scheduling, chip reconciliation → team leadership and
  operations

Where it lands: banking back-office and cash ops, compliance and AML
support roles, security operations, risk/fraud teams, customer service
leadership.

A casino CV written as tasks says "gambler-adjacent". The same CV written
as the skills above says "unflappable, numerate, compliant, calm under
pressure". Same person. Different first impression.

## Fri Sep 25 — 💬 Interactive · "Would you take a job you're overqualified for?"

---

Honest dilemma for the group:

You've been job hunting a while. An offer lands — but it's a role you're
clearly overqualified for. Lower title, less pay than your last job.

Do you take it?

The case for: income now beats income maybe, a gap shrinks, you can be
promoted from inside, and "overqualified" often means "will be great at
this" once you're in the building.

The case against: employers fear you'll leave the moment something better
appears. The pay cut stings for years, not weeks. And some people genuinely
can't do "easy" work without their spirit quietly leaving their body.

So: take it and start looking again from a stronger position, or hold out
for the right level?

And if you HAVE taken a step-down job — did it turn into a step back up,
or a trap? Real stories especially welcome.
